Must Bakkyu Starve?

A Buddha named Bakkyu is walking along a dimly lit street one night. From the shadows emerges a man with a knife in his hand. He lunges at Bakkyu. What does the Buddha do? Does he step back? Why? Would that not signal an attachment to life?

This was one of the first questions I had in mind when I began my study of Buddhism about a year ago, and under the threat of the Anti-Buddha, was among the most pernicious thorns plaguing me in the background of my study. I believe I have found some resolution now based on my own understanding and better yet, my own interpretation of the Buddha Dharma .

To deal with the problem fairly, we must understand the way Bakkyu will act purely by virtue of being a Buddha. In light of this, let's ask a harder and more interesting question: every day Bakkyu sits down for a meal. Why!? The act shows a much stronger preference for life, does it not? It is far more volitional than stepping out of the way of a knife, which could perhaps be chalked up to good reflexes. Does the act of repeatedly choosing to eat, day in and day out, not signal an attachment to life?

Let me just show my answer upfront: You do not require a self to need to eat, you only require a body. Far more importantly, a self gets in the way (often). I will try to make sense of this sentence now.

Consider Deuteronomy 31:8 - "The Lord himself goes before you and will be with you; he will never leave you nor forsake you. Do not be afraid; do not be discouraged." What a wonderful quote! I imagine a lonely Christian boy coming back home at night, reading this and immediately feeling less lonely at the idea that the Lord is with him at this moment and He will never leave him. Loneliness is (at least partially) physiological phenomena, accompanied by much constriction of the breath and pacing of the heart. And yet, through simply an idea, it can be cured, even if only for a moment.

This indicates that loneliness, beyond perhaps the physical fact of being alone, is a matter of (dare I say incorrect) conceptualisation and interpretation. How so? The Lord, unfortunately as far as I am concerned, is only in the boy's mind. Regardless of my beliefs, the Lord is not physically apprehendable by the boy. If loneliness can be cured simply by an act of imagination, then it must have been not as "real" as we make it out to be when we ourselves are lonely. If you do not take much on the Lord, when you are lonely you probably call a friend. You speak to them, you laugh and soon you feel less lonely. But you're on the phone, and your friend is not in the room. You cannot see them and you cannot touch them. You can only hear them and your mind does the rest of the work of making you feel as if you are no longer alone. As if, there is someone with you. To immediately push on this, say during the call it is suddenly revealed that the voice you had been hearing was a pre-recorded message: your friend has not been on the other side of the line this entire time. Is your loneliness back?

Loneliness is the experience of "I have no one (around me)". In a room full of people, you may be lonely because you do not register those around you as "ones" in very much the tribalistic sense ("My ones, my people, my tribe. Anyone outside, is not a "one"") the feeling probably originally developed in humans. The truth of the matter, of course, is that there are no "ones" in that tribalistic sense. A "one", a self, is a construction drawing on a narrative composed of memories, awareness, sensations, perception and the resulting mental formations and feelings.

You do not "have" a body. You are a body. Better yet, there is this body. The last sentence is all you can say if you were to stick to physically observable facts. "You", the notion and feeling of being a subject with regards to which other things and people are objects, with its whole history and narrative composed of feelings, ideas and memories, cannot be found upon investigation. You cannot find "you-ness" in your body the way you can find your hand or a ball. Worse yet, you cannot find the "hand-ness" of your hand upon touch or the "ball-ness" of the ball. At what point after you throw a chair into a fire does it stop being a chair? To be infinitely more crass, at what point after your place your brother on the funeral pyre does the body stop being your brother? The mind, through conceptualisation and then reification of those concepts, graces the world of objects (including the mind itself!) and computes the experiences of life. This is not to say that there are no objects. A "chair" as we understand it, is an assemblage (very much in the Deleuzian sense, if you care about that) of things that are themselves not "chairs" neither contain any amount of "chairness". It is only when all the things that conventionally make a chair (based on what a chair usually looks and acts like) are put together in a specific way that the mind suddenly catches the object as a "chair". This is a baby example of the teachings of emptiness and dependent arising.

The Buddha did not teach that Life is Suffering. That would be silly. The Buddha taught in the First Noble Truth that in life, there is dukkha. Even though in modern Hindi or other Indian languages, dukkha just means suffering, the Buddha used it in a very technical sense. Dukkha is better translated to English as (among other things) existential dissatisfaction. This is clarified further by the Two Arrows framework: the first arrow is whatever ("conditioned") physical phenomena are in question, physical pain among them, and the Second Arrow is all the suffering (anxiety, fear, nihilism etc) that happens after. Dukkha (that can be acted upon) is squarely about the second arrow. Why does dukkha arise? The Buddha, in the Second and Third Noble Truths, expounded the reason to be Tanha, usually translated as grasping or craving. What is grasped or craved? A self! Not necessarily of a person, but even of any "thing" (a self here broadly means the reified self-standing "thing-ness" of a thing, an essence). If you go around treating concept-nets (thrown over physical entities to grace them as "my friend Gauthama", "this ball", "my hand") as having "real" essences, you may tangle yourself in these nets and suffer for no reason. The Buddha sought to simply do away with any suffering that need not be borne. Thus he gave a Path, the Eightfold one, and this was his Fourth Noble Truth. For me, the most radical aspect of the Path is Right View: much suffering (second arrow style) can be alleviated by simply seeing life (in a way that cannot be unseen) in a different light. Seeing is different, and much harder, than knowing as a fact. Seeing is brought about through the life-changing practice that a Buddhist must undertake.

To come back to loneliness for a moment, while you cannot think or understand loneliness away, you may "see through" it. Once it is seen that there are no "ones", no tribe you "really" belong to in every possible sense of the word, the whole world of objects is free to come alive to you as "ones". All objects are now objects of attention and the whole world is lit up in its original colors ("seeing things as they are"). You recognise yourself as only a body and see all bodies (animate or inanimate) as worthy of attending to. The breath is the favourite object of many contemplative traditions, but any object works. This includes passing ideas (mental objects) such as the Lord!

A crass and silly-sounding but extremely important reductio is found for example, on doing this analysis for the loss of a parent. "You" do not "lose" your "mother". Think of all the concepts, feelings, affects and memories attached to all of these words. Even if each of those things was perfectly "real", in order to feel devastating loss one needs to perform the interpretive and narrative act of stringing together all of those things into one giant representation of your life. When my father dies, all that will have "really" happened in the moment, is that the body to which I had referred to as "my father" will no longer perform the operations usually attributed to "living" beings. If you split hairs on all the terms even in this sentence and see closely, it will be felt deeply and at a non-intellectual and pre-verbal level that one is never really "born" (as if thrust from nothing into suddenly something) and one will never really "die" (as if, just as violently, ripped from the world of existence). Being "alive" and being "dead" (consider the whole world of concepts, ideas and feelings attached to these words) are, in the end, only concepts. The physical phenomena of birth and death are quite continuous and diffuse. Gentle almost, as opposed to the ripping in and out of "existence".

As far as grief, and the larger question of what all these ideas look like in actual practice or atleast my own life, is concerned I will only say this much - all history, all memory, all sequence of events are like (but I wouldn't go as far as saying are) dreams. They are deeply internally consistent but in this moment, there is only this moment. Every moment I am born again and die again and only find my body and other bodies. To grieve, "I" and whatever it is that I am grieving, must exist only in this moment but they do not. The only moment I can truthfully bear witness to and feel the truth of is this moment. All else is much like a dream. I don't think I can really grieve because the moment of death has already passed. So have the moments of life that came before it. Now the next moment comes and then the next, and "I" and my awareness move with them, like a frog jumping from lotus pad to lotus pad. Either way, even if grief, pain or sadness occur, let them occur in the moment without a self. Let them arise and let them pass as moments come. I do not claim that this is Buddhist practice. It may be. This is my practice. I often fail at my practice.

The feeling of "I" is a net with which the mind holds together all the sensory and historical data related to the body. The following exercise can be helpful: look at a mirror and pretend if you can for a moment that you have never existed in this world. Do not think of any memory, any word you have said or any thought you have had. Best to not think at all, if you can manage it. At the same time, keep looking at your mirror image. You will soon find your body without your self. At a conventional level, there is this body. It needs to eat and it needs to shit all on its own without any intervention from "my" end. Attention and intention also do not require a self! To make a cheap quick argument, an amoeba intends, hunts and eats all without a self (atleast the kinds of selves humans may possess). Which brings us back to Bakkyu. Bakkyu The Buddha, can absolutely eat his meal everyday, because Bakkyu the Buddha is a body and a body will do its job, with or without a self. Bakkyu's Buddhahood would come under question if he were to feel secretly: "I, Bakkyu, must continue going on" which then turns into many conceptual proliferations such as ["I" "must" have this or that]. All those feelings exist purely in a narrative space and their "reality" can be seriously called into question. The point stands regardless that you do not need those feelings to go about your day. Frequently, as you may have noticed, they give you a harder time than had they not been entertained.

I have presented only a few ideas from the great perceptual machine that Buddhism provides. My point is only that one need not (actively) subscribe to the soteriology of Buddhism to benefit from the fine "seeing" that the Dharma engenders. For example karuna and upekkha (compassion and equanimity), the main ethical engines of Buddhism, arrive naturally once the self concept is seen through. What can be done with it is a different matter (eating needs violence, so Bakkyu has something to answer for).

There is much else that can be said. For example, the utility of a self, as it did not develop for no reason, is where the Anti-Buddha enters. The Anti-Buddha of Europe, as Nietzsche with all his misconceptions of Buddhism fashioned himself, saw the self as ultimately a construction yes, but a very useful one. Something to be amplified for achieving great creative heights rather than being extinguished. How right or wrong was he?

I leave these for a later time.
